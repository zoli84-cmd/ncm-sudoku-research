\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Next-Concept Modeling.} NCM \citep{kumbong2026ncm} trains a tokenizer that encodes an output, here a solved Sudoku grid, into a hierarchy of discrete codes, one codebook per level, with more codes at each finer level. The decoder sums the contributions of all levels into a cumulative state and maps it to output tokens. A generator, conditioned on the input (here the puzzle), produces the codes level by level, coarse to fine, and the puzzle remains available to the generator at every level. Two sets of codes therefore exist for every puzzle: teacher codes, obtained by encoding the reference solution, and generated codes, produced from the puzzle. We use both: the generated codes are the object of study, and the teacher codes are a control for what the solution alone determines. The NCM paper also discusses interventions on early codes and trains the generator with corruption of earlier levels; perturbing early codes is therefore not new as a method, and our contribution lies in the Sudoku-specific measurement design and its controls.

\paragraph{Codes and states.} A code index is categorical: its numerical value has no geometric meaning, and the first level of the Sudoku checkpoint is a single code with \R{model.codes} possible values. What the decoder sees after level $\ell$ is the full-length cumulative state $Z_{\le\ell}$ built from the codes of levels 1 to $\ell$. We probe this state, not the generator's internal activations; a result on one does not automatically carry over to the other.

\paragraph{Sudoku labels.} For an empty cell, the candidate mask marks the digits that do not yet appear in the cell's row, column or box. A naked single is a cell with exactly one candidate; a hidden single is a digit that has exactly one possible cell left in a row, column or box. Repeatedly filling all singles at once gives synchronous solver waves, and the wave in which a cell is first filled is its inference depth. These labels are computed from the puzzle alone, never from the solution.

\paragraph{Probing with controls.} A probe that predicts a label from a representation shows that the label is accessible to that probe, not that the model uses it \citep{belinkov2022probing}. Control tasks and matched baselines are needed to separate what the representation provides from what the probe can learn on its own \citep{hewitt2019control}, and a probe score is best read against a floor from simple inputs and a ceiling from the full input \citep{garg2026floors}. Because the candidate mask is a shallow function of the input, we pair every readout with controls that see the raw input, and with a permuted control that breaks the link between a representation and its puzzle.
